Charts first — and what the text is then for

The reading was too wordy, and the reason is specific: most of it was narrating charts that were already there. "Twelve weeks, every week" is the bars. "142 km more than before" is the ghost of the last window behind them. A sentence that describes a picture adds nothing the picture does not already say, and it adds it in a worse way — slower, and with more room to be wrong. So: the chart carries it, and the text is only what a chart cannot carry.

1Where every sentence went

The same seven facts as the earlier passes. Five of them turned out to be chart-work. Two did not.

The rule: the sentence says what the chart cannot.
A chart shows pattern: shape, trend, spread, and the athlete next to their own past. What it cannot show is a model's expectation, a model's provenance, or the absence of something. Those are the only sentences worth writing.

3What "encouraging" looks like with no adjectives

If the text cannot praise, the encouragement has to live in the drawing. It does, and it is stronger than a sentence: the chart puts the athlete next to themselves.

What carries it now

  • The ghost. The window before, behind this one. The difference is drawn, so it does not have to be said — and saying it always sounded like a verdict waiting to happen.
  • The average line. "Your typical week" as a line on the chart. Where the bars sit against it is the whole message, and it is a fact about the athlete.
  • The gap. The vertical distance between two lines on the pace chart, drawn in amber. It is the improvement; nobody has to call it one.
  • The zero line. The form's zero, drawn. Which side of it the line is on is the whole of the load's message.
  • The quiet dot. In the consistency grid, the week you did not is a pale dot. The pattern reads as a streak without a single cross.

What it costs

  • The reading is gone, and with it the thing that made the app feel like it was paying attention. That is a real loss, and it is the trade this direction makes.
  • Charts need more room than sentences. Six charts is a scroll, where one paragraph was a screen. On a phone, the charts have to earn their height.
  • Every chart must answer a question, or it is decoration. "What is this for?" has to have an answer for each of them, which is a discipline the paragraph did not need.
  • The charts are harder to get right than the words were: the ghost, the average line and the gap all have to be legible at 300 px.

5Where this leaves the voice

What the voice is now

  • It is in the drawings — the ghost, the average line, the gap, the zero line, the quiet dot. Each one is a comparison against the athlete's own past, drawn rather than said.
  • It is in the two sentences — the model's expectation, and where the model's numbers came from.
  • It is in the absence — "no zone model has been read, so there is no load to show" is still the app speaking, and it is still careful.
  • The yardstick is unchanged. You, always. There is no population to be behind.

What is still open

  • Six charts is a scroll. Which of them earn their height on a phone? The setup is the answer, but the default has to be right.
  • The charts have to be legible at 300 px — the ghost, the average and the gap all have to read at that width.
  • Does the run update stay prose? It is the one place the product writes about a run, and it is the most human thing here. Charts cannot replace it, and should not try.
  • Is anything lost that matters? The thing a paragraph did that a chart cannot: say two facts together. The chart puts them side by side. Whether that is enough is the open question, and it is the one to settle by looking rather than arguing.
