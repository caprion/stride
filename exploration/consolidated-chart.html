One chart — and the Progress wall after the cut

The prototype for the surface cut. One chart carries the pace, the heart rate and the altitude, with the series switched on and off; the Progress wall keeps eleven cards instead of twenty-four. The reasoning is in exploration/surface-inventory.md. Everything below is synthetic data, drawn in the browser, with no network and no framework.

1The one chart

One cursor, one crosshair, one tooltip. The horizontal cursor is elapsed time — not distance. Every observation the core makes about a run is a difference of times (the pace drift and the heart-rate drift are "second half minus first half", the decoupling is the heart rate's rise against the pace's, the time in a zone is seconds of moving time), and a stopped stretch covers no distance but is real elapsed time. On a distance axis "the second half" would not be the second half the core measured.

Altitude is not a pace, so it does not share the pace's scale. Metres and seconds per kilometre on one axis would flatten the pace curve into a straight line and imply a comparison the data does not support. So the altitude gets its own band — a filled, hatched area anchored to the bottom of the plot, with its own scale (its two labels live inside that band) — while the pace and the heart rate share the effort band above it. The cursor runs through both bands: the reader takes "at 22 minutes: 5:08 /km, 162 bpm, 44 m" from a single crosshair, and the ground under it stays scenery.

The default is pace and heart rate on, altitude off. The pace and the heart rate are the two rates every observation in fingerprint.rs is measured from, and they are only interesting against each other — that is the decoupling, and it is the condition story the wedge is about. The altitude is a context, not an effort signal, and it is the one toggle most people will press, because hills are what they look for. Turning it on restores the ground band; the x-axis never moves.

2What the chart decided, and why

Decided

  • One cursor, on time. Not three charts with three axes and three tooltips.
  • Altitude gets a band, not an axis. A shared axis with the pace would flatten the pace curve; the band keeps the pace curve readable and keeps the metres from pretending to be an effort.
  • The stopped stretch is a gap. The pace line breaks where the athlete was not moving, because there is no pace there (speed 0) and because that time is not time in a zone. The strip under the axis says so in words as well as marks.
  • The walking stretch is a gap too. A walk is not a running lap: the fingerprint's running_only excludes it, and so does this chart. There is no walking pace drawn at all — 11:54 /km on a 5:08-to-5:39 scale would flatten the running curve into the floor. The strip under the axis says which gap is which.
  • The heart rate runs through the stop. The reading is real; the time in a zone is not taken from it. The strip and the tooltip keep the two apart.
  • The zone bands come out of the drawing. They are a fact about time, so they live in the table under the chart, with the model and the day it was read.
  • The toggles are real controls. Native checkboxes, keyboard reachable, a visible focus ring, and the legend is drawn in the series' own shape — a dashed line, a solid line, a hatched fill — so nothing depends on colour.

Still open

  • The zone bands on the heart-rate axis. Out of the drawing here, but a reader who wants to see where their zones fall on this run will ask for them back.
  • Two thresholds for "walking". The sample-level rule in segment.rs (speed < 1.55 m/s) and the lap-level rule in fingerprint.rs (cadence < 125 spm and speed < 2.2 m/s). The chart uses the sample-level one, because that is the one the zone time and the pace bands already use — but the two should become one rule.
  • Whether the ground band stays when the altitude is off. Here it disappears and the effort band takes the height. A quieter option is a thin, always-present ground line — but then the reader is shown a shape they cannot read the scale of.
  • Whether the phone can afford a 46 px ground band. It can, just.

3The Progress wall after the cut

Twenty-four cards become eleven. Six on Progress, five on the athlete profile, in the order a runner asks: happening now → the comparison → the shape of the months → the habits. Every merged card says one thing, and the merged sources are named under it. The phone's index is the same list of cards, one row each — the areas' link lists are gone, because a second index of the same eleven things is a worse index.

The four destinations stay. Today, Runs, Progress and Body — and the athlete profile, which already ships as a fifth in frontend/src/app/destinations.ts. See the open question below.
