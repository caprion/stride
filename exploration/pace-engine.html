The pace engine — the ladder, and the physiological-cost curve

The product surface: the pace ladder, and the physiological-cost curve on its own route.

Two readings of the same athlete. The ladder (section 1) is the method's paces and heart-rate bands, on two axes. The physiological-cost curve (section 3) is a second reading on its own route, /insights/pace-curve, reached from the pace card — the user's words: "I don't want to crowd it too much, but that kind of graph is informational." Every number about the athlete on this page is read from exploration/athlete.js, the canonical athlete; the markup holds no athlete number of its own, so the prototype cannot drift from the rest of the product.

1The two-axis pace ladder

Left: the heart rate. Each rung's band is drawn on the athlete's own heart rate — the azure vertical lines are their zone floors, read from the dated zone model. Right: the pace. Each rung's pace sits on the pace scale, and the two are kept apart: the band is the method's rule, and the pace is either the method's rule or the athlete's own.

Three colours, and none of them is a verdict. Amber is the method's rule — its band and the pace its percentage produces. Azure is the athlete's own data — their race, their own pace at a heart rate, their zone floors. Grey is the engine's own derivation. The long run is one rung with two ends: it starts at the slow end of the early band and the arrow points at the faster end of the latter band, which is what the book asks for (ch. 1, ch. 7).

The threshold band. The book states the rule as the pace you could race for about an hour, and places it at the athlete's 15K-to-10-mile race pace — two paces, not one. The engine computes both from the same race time; the book's own band is narrow, and the rung draws the wider span in which the hour pace falls, with the rule named on the rung itself. The band's fast edge is the engine's 15K pace; a 45-minute time trial is the book's own route to the same band, and the threshold setup owns the athlete-facing version of the five ways the book gives.

3The physiological-cost curve

A second reading of the same athlete, on its own route. The vertical axis is the share of VO₂max a duration holds; the horizontal axis is time, on a log scale. The curve is the relation itself; the athlete's own races sit on it at the share their own pace cost; the method's sessions sit on it at their prescribed durations and paces.

How to read the curve. The curve is the share of VO₂max that a duration holds, and it is the same relation the engine uses for VDOT — so it is the same line for every athlete, and the athlete's own races are the points that place them on it. Each race sits at the share the athlete's own pace cost at their own VDOT, which is why a race from earlier sits below the line and the goal race sits on it. The method's sessions are placed by their durations and their prescribed paces. The general-aerobic day is not on the curve because the method fixes no pace and no duration for it, and heart rate is not convertible into VO₂max without the athlete's lactate relation — which is why the method's heart-rate bands are named in the legend and never drawn on this axis.

Three lines that were on the cards and are notes now. A race time is entered by the athlete; Stride does not estimate one from the training. The prediction rows carry a model with its assumption named — Riegel, exponent 1.06 — and not a forecast. Pfitzinger's bands are percentages of maximal heart rate, and this curve's axis is percentages of VO₂max.

4The states, and the two bases

What each state says

  • No race entered — the ladder is empty, and the caption names the fields: distance, time and the day of the race; the name is optional.
  • One race entered — the ladder, and the source line: which race, entered when, changed when.
  • A race entered and later replaced — the ladder redraws from the current value, and the caption says the race time was changed.
  • A race old enough to state its age — one line of fact: how many days ago the race was. A date, not a warning.
  • A rung with a missing input — the band still stands; the pace has no value, and the caption says where it would come from.
  • No race time on the curve — the curve has no value; the panel says every point on it is measured against the VDOT, and where the VDOT comes from.
  • A race time and no plan — the curve still draws, with the athlete's own races and the equivalents, and it names the plan it is missing.

What the card never does

  • It never estimates a race time from the training, and it never invents one.
  • It never scores, grades or ranks a race, and it never says the athlete is ready for a distance.
  • It never carries an age, a date of birth or a computed 180 − age.
  • It never shows a colour that means a verdict, and it never presents the athlete's own pace as the method's number.
  • It never turns an absent value into a zero.
  • On the curve it never converts heart rate into VO₂max, and it never draws the method's heart-rate bands on the VO₂max axis.

The other base is a different ladder. Pfitzinger's week runs one hard session, bands its easy days against the athlete's maximal heart rate, and paces from the athlete's own race distance. The polarised base runs two hard sessions, bands its easy days against the athlete's threshold (Zones 1–2, 72–90 % of threshold HR), and leaves the middle unused (the gray zone at 90–95 % and 100–102 % of threshold HR). The app holds one base at a time, the plan card names it, and the ladder's caption names it too — and the curve holds both, without a verdict about either.
