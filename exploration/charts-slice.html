The chart slice — what is in the athlete profile, and what is in Progress

The last prototype put paragraphs back on the screen, and that was the wrong turn. There is no reading on the screen here. Each chart carries its own meaning — in its title, in an annotation on the line, in the average line it draws — and the text under it says only what the picture cannot.

The cut is by which question a chart answers: Progress is the training — the work, over weeks. The athlete profile is the runner — the pattern, over months. Not "recent versus old": a chart's own horizon decides where it goes, and almost every chart has an obvious one.

1Progress — the training

Seven questions about the work. Every chart here answers in weeks, and every one of them has a comparison built into the drawing — an average line, a ghost of the window before, the zero the form is measured against.

2The slice, card by card

Every card keeps a home. Nothing is deleted. The reason each one lands where it does is its own horizon: a chart measured in weeks is about the training; a chart measured in months is about the runner.

3What the slice decides, and what it does not

It decides

  • Progress is seven questions about the work. The load, the volume, the comparison, the intensity, the pace, the plan, the bests.
  • The athlete profile is five questions about the runner. The shape, the habits, the schedule, the run lengths, the seasons.
  • Horizon decides the home. Weeks → Progress. Months → the profile. Two charts that argue are the pace and the zones.
  • No prose on the screen. The reading is the chart's title; the evidence is the average line, the ghost, the gap, the zero.
  • The radar's scope becomes a control — 30 / 90 / 120 days — and the two radars become one.

It does not decide

  • Whether the profile is a destination. Five destinations is a real cost. It could be a card that opens a page, and the setup a sheet inside it.
  • Where the plan and the bests sit. Both are Progress in this slice, and both could argue for the profile.
  • What the setup asks. Three questions is a guess.
  • Whether "You run every week, and the weeks get longer" is too much. A title that says a thing is still a thing being said. If the voice has to go further, the title is where it stops.
