# The voice — four passes, and the rule that makes them possible

> **Partly superseded.** The reading these passes propose was judged too
> wordy — most of it was narrating charts that were already there. The
> direction that replaced it is `exploration/charts-first.html`: the chart
> carries it, and the text is only what a chart cannot carry. The four
> passes are kept because the *voice* rules below still hold, and because
> the review that produced them is the evidence for them.

**Passes to react to, not a proposal.** Nothing here is implemented. The
numbers are illustrative.

---

## 0. The rule first

The ban list forbids `good`, `bad`, `poor`, `should`, `must`, `missed`,
`behind`, `recommended` — and the contract adds **no conclusions** and
**no commands**.

So encouragement **cannot come from an adjective about the athlete, and
cannot come from an instruction.** It has to come from somewhere else.

> **Encouragement is a fact about the athlete's own pattern, said in a
> way that lets the pattern show.**
>
> The app does not say the week was good. It says the week was the third
> in a row over 40 km, and the shape of that is the encouragement.

Three consequences, and they are the whole voice:

| | |
|---|---|
| **The yardstick is always you.** | Every comparison is against the athlete's own past. "Faster than you were in June" — never "faster than other runners". There is no population to compare against, so there is nothing to feel behind. |
| **The subject is the pattern, not the person.** | "Twelve weeks, every week" is about a streak. "You are consistent" is about a character. The first is checkable; the second is a verdict wearing a compliment. |
| **No instructions, ever.** | Not "ease off", not "you should rest", not "time for a recovery week". A scenario may say *what the model expects if nothing changes* — that is a fact about a model, not an order. |

And the thing that makes it work: **a coach does not say "good job". A
coach says "third week in a row over 40" — and the praise is in the
counting.**

## 1. The same six facts, four ways

The facts, so the passes can be judged on wording and nothing else:

1. 300 km over 12 weeks, 35 runs — 142 km more than the 12 weeks before.
2. A run every week for 12 weeks.
3. At 151 bpm: 7:26 /km last week, 7:31 /km six weeks ago.
4. Acute load 42, chronic 29, form −12.7, ratio 1.44.
5. The ratio is expected back inside 0.8–1.3 in 1 day; the form turns positive in 4 days.
6. Z4–Z5 was 67% of the moving time in the window before, 53% in this one.

---

### Pass 1 — plain

*The baseline: the core's sentences, read out in order. No warmth, no
cold. This is what the product sounds like today, minus the labels.*

> You have run 300 km in 12 weeks, 142 km more than the 12 weeks before.
>
> You have run every week for 12 weeks.
>
> Your pace at 151 bpm was 7:26 /km in the last week and 7:31 /km six weeks ago.
>
> Your acute load is 42 and your chronic load is 29. The form is −12.7. The ratio is 1.44.
>
> The acute-to-chronic ratio is expected back inside 0.8 to 1.3 in 1 day. The form turns positive in 4 days.
>
> Z4–Z5 was 67% of your moving time in the window before and 53% in this one.

**Reads as:** a spec sheet in sentences. Accurate, and it asks the reader
to do everything.

**What it gets right:** nothing is judged, nothing is ordered, every
number has its period.

---

### Pass 2 — warm

*Encouragement from the pattern. Still no adjectives about the athlete.*

> Twelve weeks, thirty-five runs, 300 km — and 142 km more than the twelve weeks before.
>
> Every one of those weeks had a run in it.
>
> At the same heart rate you are quicker than you were: 7:26 /km at 151 bpm last week, against 7:31 six weeks ago.
>
> The load has gone the other way, though. 42 acute against 29 chronic, and the form has been negative for three weeks — more than your body has been used to. With a week off, the ratio is expected back inside 0.8 to 1.3 by tomorrow, and the form positive in four days.
>
> The hard running has come down: Z4–Z5 was 67% of your time in the window before and 53% in this one.

**Reads as:** a friend who has been looking at the same data. Warm without
being congratulatory, because every warm thing is a pattern.

**The "though"** is the load line's turn. It is the one place the facts
pull in two directions, and saying so plainly is more honest — and more
engaging — than presenting six numbers in a row.

---

### Pass 3 — colleague

*A fellow runner talking. Shorter sentences, second person, contractions.
The register the run update already uses.*

> Twelve weeks straight, and 142 km more than the twelve weeks before it.
>
> Same heart rate, more distance — at 151 bpm you were 7:31 six weeks ago and 7:26 last week.
>
> The load's the other way. You've been doing more than your body's used to: 42 acute against 29 chronic, so the form's been sitting below zero for three weeks. A week off and the ratio's back inside the band tomorrow; the form turns in four days.
>
> The one thing that's come down is the hard running — Z4–Z5 was two thirds of your time before, about half now.

**Reads as:** the voice of the run update, but about the training.

**What changes:** the sentences get shorter and the *timing* changes —
the load arrives after the good news rather than in numeric order, and
"the one thing that's come down" frames the zone change as the one
*reversal* in the set. That is not a judgement; it is orientation.

---

### Pass 4 — short

*What fits on a phone above the fold: three lines, then the picture.*

> Twelve weeks, every week, 300 km — 142 km more than the twelve weeks before.
>
> Same heart rate, quicker: 7:26 /km at 151 bpm, against 7:31 six weeks ago.
>
> More load than your body's used to: 42 acute against 29 chronic. A week off and the ratio's back inside the band tomorrow.
>
> *\[ the picture \]*

**Reads as:** a headline, a fact, a caveat, a picture.

**Why three lines and not one:** each line is a *different idea* — how
much, whether it is working, how hard it is right now — and one line
each is what makes a phone screen scannable. Collapsing them into one
sentence loses the structure.

## 2. What I would not write, and why

| written | verdict |
|---|---|
| "You've had a great training block!" | judgement + patronising. The 300 km says it. |
| "You're fitter than 8 weeks ago." | **allowed and wanted** — this is the core's own `statement.progress.fitter`, and it is a comparison, not a verdict. It needs its evidence beside it: *"at the same heart rate, 24 s/km faster"*. |
| "You should ease off this week." | a command. The scenario already says what a week off would do; that is a fact about a model. |
| "Your fitness is improving nicely." | an evaluation of a trend, plus an adverb doing the work the number should do. |
| "You missed your long run on Saturday." | `missed` is banned, and it is a verdict about a session. |
| "Consistency is your strength." | about character, not pattern. "Every week for twelve weeks" is the same message and is checkable. |
| "At 151 bpm you were 7:26 /km last week, against 7:31 six weeks ago." | **exactly right.** A fact, with its period, that a runner reads as good news without the app saying so. |
| "Z4–Z5 was 67% of your time before and 53% now." | right. A direction, not a judgement about whether it should be higher. |

## 3. The consistency grid — the thing in your screenshot

Dreeve's "Challenge consistency" is a grid: **your challenges** down the
side, **weeks** across, a tick or a cross in each cell.

The *format* is good and it is worth having: it is your own goals, it is
your own history, and it shows a pattern in one glance that no single
number shows. Two things are wrong with it for this product.

**The crosses.** A row of red ✗ beside a goal *you set yourself* is the
opposite of encouraging, and it is a verdict about a week. Nothing in
this product is red, and `missed` is on the ban list. The constructive
version keeps the grid and drops the red:

> the cell that was not done is a quiet dot, not a cross.

**The framing.** "Complete a half marathon run" as a *challenge* is a
thing you either did or did not, and the grid then counts failures. The
encouraging framing is the pattern:

> "You have done a half marathon in 9 of the last 12 weeks."

Same facts. One is a scoreboard of misses; the other is a streak.

**And it connects to the setup.** If the challenges are the athlete's
own, the reading has a subject:

> "You wanted to be consistent about a half marathon. You have run one in 9 of the last 12 weeks — and this was the third in a row."

That sentence is the voice the whole product is missing: constructive,
not patronising, not judgemental, not an order, and it is *about the
thing the athlete cares about*.

## 4. The setup

If the reading is about what the athlete cares about, then something has
to say what they care about. That is the setup, and it is three questions
asked once:

**1 · What do you want to know about your training?**

- Is my fitness moving?
- Am I doing more than my body is used to?
- Am I consistent?
- Am I quick enough for what I'm aiming at?

Each one selects a **fact group** the reading draws from. Checking
"fitness" and "consistency" and nothing else gives a two-line reading;
checking everything gives the full thing.

**2 · What do you want to be consistent about?**

The challenges — 5 km, 10 km, half marathon, 100 km a month, 2000 m of
climbing. The athlete's own words for their own goals. These become the
rows of the consistency view, and they become a subject for the reading.

**3 · How much do you want to read?**

- *A line or two* — the phone's default.
- *A short paragraph* — the desktop's default.
- *The whole thing* — every fact group, every chart, the full page.

## 5. The open questions in the voice itself

1. **Do we want "though"?** Pass 2 and 3 both pivot on it. It is the one
   place the facts disagree with each other, and naming that is honest.
   But it can read as scolding if the pivot lands on the wrong fact.
   Alternative: order the facts so the tension is visible without a
   conjunction.
2. **How warm is warm enough?** Pass 2 is the warmest that still says
   nothing the numbers do not say. Is there room for one more degree, or
   is that the ceiling?
3. **Should the reading ever mention the athlete's plan?** The plan is
   the athlete's own intent, and comparing the training against it is
   factual. But it is also the easiest place to slide into "you fell
   short". My instinct is: no, not yet — and the setup's challenges are
   the safer version of the same idea, because the athlete wrote them.
4. **Does the reading speak in the first person?** "You have run" or
   "you've run"? The run update says "you". Consistency says "you".
